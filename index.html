    Part 0

    The big picture

    Why AI systems are hard to judge by eye

    Outputs are distributions

    The same input can produce different outputs. One trial is one sample.

    Fluent ≠ correct

    The model can confidently say something while the underlying action failed. Narration can differ from actual state.

    Averages hide tails

    A 99% average can hide a rare but catastrophic failure.

    What an eval is not

    Not one magic metric

    Correctness, safety, latency, tool execution and retrieval each need different evidence.

    Not merely prompt testing

    The fix may belong in retrieval, tool execution, state management, code, the model, the spec, or the eval itself.

    Not a guardrail

    A guardrail intervenes ("stop this action"). An eval measures ("did this action happen correctly?").

    A number isn't automatically objective

    92% of what? Judged how? On which cases? By whom? Metrics move judgment upstream; they don't remove it.

    Claim boundary

    Principle

    A result extends only as far as the evidence behind it: its cases, environment, grader, and sampling.

    Which claims does this evidence support?

    Evidence: 94% pass on 200 English refund cases, staging environment, one trial per case, graded by an LLM judge validated on 80 expert labels.

    Part 2

    Core vocabulary

    Case

    A reproducible situation being tested. Not merely a prompt: user input, initial state, expected behavior, forbidden behavior, severity, and metadata/slices.

    Build up a case

    Toggle fields on. A prompt alone is the first line only.

    Trial

    One execution of a case. Because systems are stochastic, the same case can pass on trial 1 and fail on trial 2.

    Trace

    What happened during the execution: request, retrieval, model, tool call, tool result, state change, final response. The main diagnostic artifact.

    Step through a trace

    Case: "The charger was missing. Refund just that part."

    Holdout

    Evidence you deliberately haven't "spent" while developing. Repeatedly testing on the same data lets you optimize against it; the holdout is the final, untouched check.

    Case design

    Negative space is first-class

    Define what must not happen (refund whole order; claim success when the tool failed) as carefully as what should.

    Where to look for evidence

    Grade the closest observable to the real outcome

    Case buckets, and why slices must be reported separately

    How a blended average hides a risk slice

    Change how much of the suite is high-impact. Watch the headline number stay comfortable.

    Where cases come from

    Production logs, domain experts, incidents, synthetic generation, benchmarks, red-team cases.

    Synthetic data warning

    "Generate 1,000 diverse questions" tends to yield one template with minor wording changes. Generate from a coverage plan (dimensions × values) instead.

    Circularity warning

    Don't let one model generate the cases, the answers, and the judgments. Its blind spots become your eval's blind spots.

    Dataset leakage

    Cases need independence across user, document, repository, and time. Random row splits can leak near-duplicates into both dev and holdout. A time split (before/after) also helps detect drift.

    Random row split vs group split

    Part 7

    LLM as a judge

    Core idea

    An LLM judge is a measurement instrument. Instruments have noise (decisions flip on irrelevant changes) and bias (systematic preferences). Measure both.

    What's wrong with the naive judge

    Question: ... Response A: ... Response B: ... Which is better? Reply A or B.
    • No rubric: the judge invents its own meaning of "better".
    • No tie: two equally good answers must still be ranked.
    • Hidden preferences: longer, more polished, particular styles.

    Judge lab: position and verbosity bias

    Each simulated pair is A = correct and concise, B = wrong and verbose. Every pair is judged in both orders.

    Better judge design

    1. Correctness-only rubric
    2. Atomic criteria (one question each)
    3. Length and style explicitly out of scope
    4. Tie allowed
    5. Judge both orders; if they disagree, record a tie and flag it

    This removes position bias by construction, at roughly 2× judge calls.

    Other judge biases Added

    Self-preference

    Judges tend to favor outputs from their own model family. Use a different family from the system under test when possible.

    Format / authority

    Markdown, citations and confident tone can win regardless of correctness.

    Leniency

    Pointwise judges often skew toward PASS or high scores. Require evidence for each criterion.

    Reasoning order

    Ask for the reasoning before the verdict, not after, so the verdict isn't rationalized.

    Validate your judge

    Why raw agreement lies

    Compare a judge against 100 expert labels. Try the lazy judge at a high pass base rate.

    Interview angle Added Name the metrics: false-pass rate (judge says PASS, expert says FAIL; the dangerous one), false-fail rate, true-pass/true-fail rates, and Cohen's κ = (observed − chance agreement) / (1 − chance agreement). Validate on a held-out labeled set, not the examples you tuned the judge prompt on.
    Part 8

    Statistics and trust

    pass@k vs pass^k

    Same p, very different stories

    pass@k: at least one succeeds

    1 − (1 − p)^k

    pass^k: all k succeed

    p^k

    pass@k asks

    Can I get a successful attempt? Fits coding with tests you can check, or best-of-n sampling.

    pass^k asks

    Can I succeed consistently? Fits customer-facing agents where every conversation must work. Popularized by τ-bench.
    Precision note Fixed The formulas assume independent trials and a known p. Real evals estimate from n samples with c correct; HumanEval's unbiased estimator is pass@k = 1 − C(n−c, k) / C(n, k).

    Confidence intervals Added

    "92%" means nothing without its uncertainty. Report an interval. Wilson is the standard choice for pass rates because it behaves well near 0% and 100%.

    Interval calculator

    Rule of three

    Zero failures in n trials → 95% upper bound on the failure rate ≈ 3/n. To claim <0.1% you need ≈3,000 failure-free trials.

    Comparing A vs B

    Run both versions on the same cases and compare per case (paired). Use McNemar's test or a paired bootstrap; count wins, losses and ties rather than eyeballing two averages.

    Severity

    11 failures are not 11 equal failures

    Counts are observations. Weights and severity priorities are product decisions.Keep critical-failure rates separate from ordinary quality averages.

    Evals decay

    "A benchmark is an instrument, not an institution." Causes of decay: broken cases, underspecified tasks, contamination, changing system behavior, changing production environment, and metrics losing connection to real outcomes. The suite itself needs maintenance.

    Interview angle Added Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Holdouts, refreshed cases and periodic human audits are the defenses. Criteria drift: graders' definition of "good" shifts as they see more outputs, so re-check rubrics against fresh traces.

    RAG metrics

    Retrieval (component)

    Precision@k: relevant in top k ÷ k. Recall@k: relevant in top k ÷ all relevant. Hit rate@k: any relevant in top k. MRR: 1 ÷ rank of first relevant. nDCG: rewards relevant docs ranked higher, supports graded relevance.

    Generation

    Faithfulness / groundedness: every claim supported by retrieved context. Answer relevance: addresses the question. Context precision/recall: whether the right evidence was retrieved (Ragas naming). Citation accuracy: cited source actually supports the claim.

    Retrieval metrics calculator

    Click documents to mark them relevant. The corpus has 3 relevant documents in total.

    Hallucination vs retrieval failure maps to this split: if the right doc wasn't retrieved, it's a retrieval problem (Part 10); if it was retrieved and the answer contradicts it, it's a faithfulness problem.

    Judging modes and scales

    Pointwise

    Score one output against a rubric. Scales to monitoring. Prone to leniency.

    Pairwise

    Compare two outputs. More sensitive for A/B decisions. Needs order swapping.

    Reference-guided

    Judge sees a gold answer. Better accuracy, but inherits the reference's flaws.
    Binary vs Likert Prefer binary pass/fail per atomic criterion. 1–5 scales drift between graders, the middle values are poorly defined, and "3.7 average" doesn't tell you what to fix. If you need nuance, split the criterion.

    Human evaluation

    Write annotation guidelines with examples, have at least two annotators label an overlap set, and measure inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ for two raters, Krippendorff's α or Fleiss' κ for more). Low agreement usually means the criterion is underspecified, not that the annotators are careless.

    Contamination

    Public benchmark items may be in a model's training data, inflating scores. Defenses: private or freshly written cases, time-split cases created after the model's cutoff, and canary strings in held-out data.

    Online evaluation vocabulary

    Shadow

    New version runs on real traffic; outputs are logged, not shown to users.

    Canary

    New version serves a small slice of users, with rollback ready.

    A/B test

    Randomized split with a pre-registered primary metric and sample size.

    Guardrail metrics

    Metrics that must not regress (safety, latency, cost) even if the primary improves. Different from a runtime guardrail.

    Evals in the dev workflow

    • Eval-driven development: write the failing case before the fix, like TDD.
    • CI gates: a small fast regression suite on every PR; the full suite nightly or before release.
    • Version everything: dataset, prompt, model, judge prompt, and harness, so a score is reproducible.
    • Tooling (choose last): promptfoo, DeepEval, Ragas, Inspect, OpenAI Evals, LangSmith, Braintrust, Langfuse, Arize Phoenix.
    Ref

    The seven things to remember

    1. Define the decision first. What will this eval decide?
    2. A case is more than a prompt: input + state + expected + forbidden + severity.
    3. Grade the closest observable to reality. DB state beats agent narration.
    4. Separate criterion, grader, metric.
    5. Your evaluator can be wrong. Product failure or eval failure?
    6. Debug from the first divergence. Find the first NO.
    7. Every production failure should improve the suite. Incident → case → regression.

    Final mental model

    Don't ask "how do I evaluate my LLM?" Ask: what decision am I making, what evidence would convince me, and how can I obtain it repeatedly?

    AI evals = turning AI behavior into trustworthy evidence for a decision.
